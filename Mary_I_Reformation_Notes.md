# Mary I (1553–1558): Case Studies by Factor

---

# Protestant Ideas

### The Burning of Protestants and Foxe's Book of Martyrs
The burnings are the single strongest evidence that Protestant ideas had generated genuine, deep personal conviction among ordinary English people by Mary's reign — and that persecution entrenched rather than eliminated that conviction.
- 224 Protestants were burned over 44 months (roughly five per month), concentrated mainly in London and Norwich; the total is sometimes cited as 300 including later counts
- First Protestant burned at the stake: 4 February 1555; Bishop John Hooper burned 9 February 1555; Bishops Ridley and Latimer burned at Oxford, October 1555; Archbishop Cranmer burned March 1556
- The martyrs' conduct at the stake is documented in extraordinary detail by John Foxe: John Hullier in Cambridge caught a copy of the Communion Service thrown into the fire, read it joyfully until fire and smoke blinded him, then pressed it to his heart; Hooper's arms eventually fell off as he continued to knock his breast; George Eagles was described as a tailor who "manfully fought under the banner of Christ" and was "foully mangled" by a bungling executioner yet "stayed steadfast in the midst of his torments"
- This conduct is direct primary evidence of deeply held Protestant conviction among ordinary laypeople, not just clergy or elite politicians
- Foxe's Acts and Monuments (Book of Martyrs), first published 1563 during Elizabeth's reign, recorded these deaths in graphic, deliberate detail to shape opinion against Catholicism; Elizabeth ordered a copy placed in every parish church
- The lesson note on Foxe identifies the key historiographical problem: "Protestant propaganda which demonised Mary and the Catholic Church began under Elizabeth I... and built up over later centuries as Anglicanism was established. This view dominated public and to a large extent academic interpretations of Mary for centuries, and has only recently been challenged"
- At the time of the burnings themselves there was no Protestant rebellion — most Protestant leaders fled abroad rather than mounting armed resistance

### Cranmer's Recantation and Retraction
Cranmer's case specifically illustrates both the fragility and the ultimate resilience of Protestant conviction under extreme pressure, and Mary's personal religious motivation overriding legal process.
- Cranmer, under sustained pressure as an old and broken man facing death by fire, recanted and accepted the Pope and the Mass — a "tremendous propaganda victory for Mary"
- As the law then stood, recantation should have spared his life
- Both Foxe's account and Mary's own letters make clear she personally intervened to order that Cranmer should burn regardless of his recantation
- At the stake Cranmer publicly retracted his recantation, thrusting into the fire first the hand that had signed it, crying "this hand hath offended" — transforming a propaganda victory into a martyrdom story of extraordinary power
- This sequence shows Protestant ideas operating at the most extreme personal level: Cranmer's final act was not politically calculated but a statement of theological conviction made in full knowledge of the consequences

### The Regional Persistence of Protestantism
The survival of an entrenched Protestant minority in specific regions shows Protestant ideas had taken genuine root and could not be extirpated by state coercion alone.
- Protestantism persisted as "an entrenched and substantial" minority specifically in London and the South East — the same regional geography as the earlier Lollard and early evangelical networks, suggesting a traceable continuous popular base rather than a purely elite or top-down identity
- Only in London and the county of Essex were there any widespread enthusiasm for Protestantism among both authorities and ordinary people (Turvey and Heard)
- Pendrill notes the spread of the English Bible meant Protestantism had taken root in some areas and among many of the nobility and gentry — even if the majority of the country remained willing to follow the monarch's religious lead
- Early Elizabethan bishops faced greater difficulty getting parishes to comply with mandates to remove Catholic objects than any of their Tudor predecessors had faced (Marshall) — suggesting ordinary people's religious convictions had hardened and clarified during Mary's reign, on both sides

# Financial Motivation

### The Irreversibility of the Monastic Land Sales
The financial consequences of Henry's dissolution created a structural constraint on what Mary could achieve, regardless of her religious intentions — the strongest financial case study for the whole reign.
- Mary was forced to moderate her demands for the return of monastic lands, in the face of opposition from the gentry and landowners who had purchased them since 1536
- This moderation was a crucial political concession: without it she would have lost the parliamentary and propertied-class support that made the religious restoration possible
- Pendrill's judgement: Mary "moderated her demands to return monastic lands in the face of opposition, ensuring she did not push too hard too soon. She also resisted papal control when Paul IV tried to recall Pole to Rome... This ensured the support of the gentry and landowners in Parliament, so ensured the success of Mary's counter-Reformation"
- The gentry and landowners who had profited from the dissolution thus directly constrained how far a Catholic restoration could go, even under a genuinely committed Catholic monarch with full parliamentary support
- This is the clearest demonstration in the whole period that the financial consequences of Henry's Reformation outlasted his reign and shaped the limits of what all subsequent monarchs could do

### The Spanish Marriage — Financial Dimensions
The marriage to Philip II had financial implications that both supported and undermined Mary's position.
- Philip II was, as contemporary observers noted, the richest monarch in Europe — the Spanish alliance brought England into the orbit of a vast empire spanning Europe and the Americas
- The marriage contract guaranteed that any heir of Mary and Philip would inherit the Low Countries as well as England, potentially securing a significant continental commercial asset for England
- However the marriage also drew England into Philip's war against France, at considerable financial cost, ultimately resulting in the loss of Calais in January 1558 — England's last continental territory, held for 211 years
- Although English pride was seriously damaged, Pendrill argues the financial impact on trade was limited — "the loss of Calais was not a national disaster" since English merchants to the continent were already using other ports, and the Exchequer was not seriously affected

# Power

### Mary's Accession and the Defeat of Lady Jane Grey
Mary's accession in 1553 is the clearest demonstration in her reign that dynastic legitimacy outweighed religious preference as a political force — even convinced Protestants supported her over Jane Grey.
- When Edward died and Northumberland proclaimed Jane queen, even the ambassadors of Mary's own cousin the Emperor Charles V initially accepted Jane as queen
- Mary acted immediately: withdrew to Framlingham Castle in her East Anglian heartland, raising men from her own estates and friendly neighbouring gentry
- Even convinced Protestants welcomed her; she was proclaimed queen from as far afield as Gloucester to Yorkshire
- She mustered at least 15,000 men without a single blow being struck; Lady Jane Grey's support had lasted only nine days
- Mary entered London on 3 August 1553: entered from the east at Aldgate, rode through streets hung with tapestries to the Tower, crowds weeping tears of joy, quoting Romans: "If God be with us, who can be against us?"
- Pendrill's assessment: "In the event very few people supported Jane — the majority of the nobility and gentry, sensing the way things were going, flocked to Mary"; the English political nation chose between Catholic and Protestant and chose the legitimate Tudor heir
- This outcome directly challenges any reading of 1553 England as a substantially Protestant country: the people rejected a Protestant candidate in favour of a known and committed Catholic

### The Restoration of Catholicism Through Parliament
Mary's systematic use of parliamentary statute to restore Catholicism shows effective deployment of constitutional power, making her restoration more durable than the ad hoc, contested process of Henry's original break.
- Parliament passed a Statute of Repeal in October 1553, repealing the Act of Uniformity and all religious legislation since the Act of Six Articles of 1539
- Administration of the sacraments reverted to how they had been in the last year of Henry VIII's reign — less than seven years before
- England was reunited with Rome in November 1554; Cardinal Pole, who had narrowly lost the papal election to Julius III by two votes, was empowered as Papal Legate and brought England formally back under papal authority
- The return to Rome was described as "euphoric and enthusiastic" — "the Reformation had begun with what appears to have been a genuinely popular reaction"
- Mary also: established an Index of prohibited books (1555); introduced a death penalty for possession of forbidden books (1558); set up seminaries in every diocese to train new priests; deliberately rejected help from the Jesuits to avoid association with foreign, violent Catholicism
- Pendrill's three-part explanation of success: she acted with freely given consent of Parliament; she acted without undue speed; she was not unduly vengeful toward Protestant enemies
- The majority of bishops and lesser clergy conformed; most of the 800 beneficed clergy deprived of their livings were deprived for being married, not for doctrinal objection

### Wyatt's Rebellion and Mary's Guildhall Speech
Mary's handling of Wyatt's Rebellion is the most striking exercise of personal royal authority in her reign — she faced down an armed uprising through rhetoric alone.
- In early 1554 news reached Mary of a conspiracy; Sir Thomas Wyatt raised over 3,000 men in Kent, marching on London to depose Mary and replace her with Elizabeth with an English husband
- Mary was isolated in London with few loyal forces; the Duke of Coventry encountered outright hostility in Hertfordshire; Croft failed to raise any troops in Devon
- On 1 February 1554, Mary went in procession to the Guildhall and addressed the citizens of London directly in "a deep, resonant, great voice"
- Her speech: "I am your lawful crowned queen... With this ring, I wedded my realm. It has never left my finger and it will never leave my finger... I love you, my people, as a mother loves her child. And I swear to you, that I never intended, nor ever shall, marry without the consent of my council, my parliament, and you, my people"
- The city held firm; gates were shut in Wyatt's face; trapped, he surrendered
- Rebel leaders were swiftly executed; Elizabeth was briefly imprisoned in the Tower
- Only 350 Kentish men were executed — Pendrill notes this was deliberately limited punishment, suggesting moderation even in victory
- The rebellion's failure indicated limited popular support for either hard-line Protestantism or opposition to the Spanish marriage; Pendrill: "there was little opposition to the Spanish marriage and the return of Catholicism, and little support for hard line Protestantism or Puritanism"

### The Burning of Protestants as an Exercise of State Power
The burnings show state coercive power being used at its most extreme — and ultimately failing to achieve religious uniformity, demonstrating the limits of power without popular consent or dynastic continuity.
- Mary was "wholly and personally committed to the policy of burning heretics alive" — "not that she was especially cruel and vindictive, far from it. She actually thought she was doing good and rescuing England from itself"
- Her reasoning was theological: heresy was treason against God, the worst possible crime deserving the worst possible punishment
- Mary personally overrode the law to ensure Cranmer burned despite his legal right to be spared upon recantation — both Foxe's account and Mary's own letters make this explicit
- The burnings were "extreme by English standards" but represented only five executions per month and were moderate compared to continental equivalents; no Protestant rebellion occurred at the time
- Stephen Gardiner advised caution and restraint; after his death the programme became "too hasty," losing the political judgement that had kept the earlier phase from provoking revolt
- The ultimate counter-productivity of the burnings occurred not immediately but through long-term propaganda: Foxe's Book of Martyrs permanently shaped English national identity against Catholicism and Catholic power
- Duffy's Fires of Faith thesis argues the persecution was "to a considerable extent successful" in the short term — challenging any simple "failure" narrative and showing coercive power came genuinely close to working, stopped only by Mary's premature death

### The Loss of Calais and Mary's Death
Mary's final years show the compounding consequences of power exercised without sufficient independent political base — military, dynastic, and personal failures converging.
- Philip returned to England but stayed only four months; Mary believed she was pregnant for a second time, wrote a will outlining the Catholic England her unborn child would inherit; by May 1558, again no baby had been born
- England declared war on France on Philip's behalf in June 1557; the Earl of Pembroke captured St Quentin but the English arrived too late to benefit; in January 1558 the French captured Calais, England's last continental territory held for 211 years
- Pendrill: the loss of Calais "is often seen as a fitting climax to a misguided and disastrous Marian foreign policy" but argues "such a view is clearly exaggerated" — English trade was not seriously affected, national pride was wounded more than the economy
- Mary died 17 November 1558, aged 42 — possibly ovarian cancer, possibly the influenza epidemic that had already killed many subjects; her husband recorded "a moderate grief" at her passing
- She was not buried with her mother Catherine as she had requested but with Elizabeth; the magnificent tomb over their shared grave is a monument only to Elizabeth
- Pendrill's verdict: Mary's failure was not inevitable — "Had Mary lived longer, or had she produced a Catholic heir, the English Church would have remained Catholic for the foreseeable future"; to blame her for Elizabeth's Protestant succession is "to read history backwards"
