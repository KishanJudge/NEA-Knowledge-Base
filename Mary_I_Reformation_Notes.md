# Mary I (1553–1558)

## The Marian Counter-Reformation

**Summary:** Becoming Queen in 1553, Mary, Edward's devoutly Catholic sister, was always going to have a tough time undoing twenty years of reform. Although Protestantism remained patchy and its followers a minority, this minority was entrenched and substantial, at least in London and the South East. Mary did her best, reinstating Catholic doctrines and rites and replacing altars and images, but she handicapped herself by martyring almost 300 ordinary men and women, as well as bigger names like Cranmer. The burnings were unpopular and immensely counter-productive.

**Protestant Ideas**
- Protestantism's described persistence as an "entrenched and substantial" minority, specifically in London and the South East, directly echoes the same regional geography linked earlier to Lollardy, reinforcing the idea of a real, traceable Protestant popular base rather than a purely state-imposed identity
- The burnings of Protestants fuelled further Protestant propaganda rather than eliminating dissent, suggesting persecution entrenched rather than removed Protestant conviction among the population — evidence that genuine belief, not just political convenience, was now driving Protestant identity in England

**Financial Motivation**
- No specific new financial detail is given for Mary's reign in the source beyond the continuity implied by previously confiscated Church lands remaining in lay hands from Edward's reign

**Power**
- Mary "did her best" to reinstate Catholic doctrine and rites and replace altars and images, a genuine state-led religious policy, not merely cosmetic
- She "handicapped herself" by martyring almost 300 ordinary people and high-profile figures like Cranmer; the burnings were unpopular and counter-productive, illustrating how state coercion failed to achieve religious uniformity, in contrast to how coercion under Henry had at least succeeded in forcing political compliance

## Mary's Spanish Marriage, the Loss of Calais, and Her Death

**Summary:** Mary compounded her errors by marrying Philip II of Spain, son of Charles V, who had so successfully thwarted Henry's own divorce attempt in 1527. Burning bodies, Spanish courtiers, and Philip's "awful English" all fuelled further Protestant propaganda and confirmed fears of the Catholic menace that had been threatened since 1534. Fighting France on Philip's behalf, Mary's loss of Calais in 1558 — England's last territory in France — helped turn distrust into hatred and xenophobia. Tension mounted, with Thomas Wyatt rebelling in Kent, and religious civil war seemed not too far away. However, chance rolled the dice once more: after two phantom pregnancies, Mary died childless in November 1558, leaving the throne to her only heir, Elizabeth, Anne Boleyn's daughter.

**Power**
- Her marriage to Philip II directly linked Mary's reign back to the same Habsburg dynasty that had blocked Henry's annulment decades earlier, a pointed historical irony
- The loss of Calais, England's last French territory, fought for on behalf of a foreign king, turned domestic distrust into outright hatred and xenophobia
- Thomas Wyatt's rebellion in Kent showed tension mounting toward what the source explicitly frames as the brink of religious civil war
- Mary's death, after two phantom pregnancies, is explicitly framed by the source as a moment where "chance rolled the dice once more" — a contingent, accidental outcome rather than an inevitable one, leaving the throne to Elizabeth

## Historiographical Reassessment: Was Mary's Reign a Failure?

**Summary:** Mary's reign represents a major challenge for any account of the Reformation that assumes a straightforward, unidirectional Protestant triumph, and historian Peter Marshall identifies it as remaining "a major historiographical battlefield." Eamon Duffy's chapter on Marian Catholicism in The Stripping of the Altars was, according to Marshall, "perhaps the most contentious part" of that entire book, since it offered an unusually optimistic assessment of Mary's religious programme, in line with similarly positive reassessments by historians such as Tom Mayer, Lucy Wooding, and Bill Wizeman. Duffy's account was criticised at the time for largely passing over the feature of the reign most associated with it in popular memory: the campaign of religious persecution that burned around 300 Protestant men and women between 1555 and 1558. That criticism evidently struck home, since Duffy later made the burnings the central theme of a fresh set of lectures, delivered in Cambridge in late 2007 and published in 2009 as Fires of Faith: Catholic England under Mary Tudor. His thesis there, that the persecution policy was both inevitable, given the circumstances, and to a considerable extent successful in its own terms, is described by Marshall as "likely to be highly controversial."

Marshall goes further still, offering his own reassessment of what Mary's reign actually achieved. He argues that, although the Marian Counter-Reformation clearly failed in the narrow sense (it did not permanently restore Catholicism as England's official religion), its real, if unintended, achievement was to consolidate the forces of Protestant reform and lay the basis for "a lasting anti-Catholic myth of Protestant national identity." But Marshall identifies a second, less obvious achievement that he considers genuinely significant rather than merely counterfactual: Mary's reign ensured the subsequent survival of Catholicism in England as a permanent minority presence, embedding religious division and plurality as a lasting feature of English political and cultural life. This runs directly counter to the older thesis of historian John Bossy, who had argued that English Catholicism was effectively wiped out by around 1570 and only rebuilt from scratch by Jesuit missionaries in the second half of Elizabeth's reign. Marshall's evidence for his alternative view is that the thorough re-Catholicisation of both the Church hierarchy and the universities between 1553 and 1558 produced a large flood of highly educated clerical exiles to Louvain and other continental centres once Elizabeth came to the throne; this Elizabethan Catholic exile, he notes, was "a much larger and more politically formidable phenomenon" than the earlier Marian Protestant exile under Mary herself had been. He also points to a striking piece of administrative evidence: early Elizabethan bishops faced greater difficulty getting parishes to comply with official mandates to remove or destroy forbidden Catholic objects than any of their Tudor predecessors had faced, which Marshall argues suggests ordinary people by this point had a clearer and more settled sense of what was actually at stake religiously than they had had back in the 1530s and 1540s.

**Protestant Ideas**
- The fact that Duffy's optimistic reassessment of Marian Catholicism provoked such strong pushback over its treatment of the burnings shows how central state persecution remains to any credible account of why Protestantism ultimately survived and triumphed in England, regardless of genuine Catholic popular support during Mary's reign
- Marshall's point about the greater difficulty Elizabethan bishops faced in enforcing compliance, compared to their Tudor predecessors, is significant evidence that ordinary religious conviction, on one side or the other, had hardened and deepened considerably by the late 1550s compared to the more fluid, uncertain situation of two decades earlier

**Power**
- Duffy's Fires of Faith thesis, that Mary's persecution was "to a considerable extent successful," directly challenges any simple narrative that the burnings were a pure policy failure; if Marshall and Duffy are right, coercive state power came genuinely close to working as a tool of religious uniformity, and only Mary's premature, childless death prevented a longer test of that policy
- Marshall's argument that Mary's reign permanently entrenched religious plurality, rather than simply failing outright, reframes the entire period 1520-1603 in a way directly relevant to the essay: it suggests no single set of ideas, Protestant or Catholic, ever achieved total, lasting victory through power alone, and that the genuine survival of minority religious conviction on both sides constrained what any monarch's power could ultimately achieve
