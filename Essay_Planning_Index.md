# Essay Planning Index: Best Evidence by Factor

For the question "'Protestant ideas were responsible for the development of the Reformation in the years 1520–1603' How far do you agree with this statement?" — this index ranks, for each monarch, which sections or case studies provide the strongest evidence for each of the three essay factors (Protestant Ideas, Financial Motivation, Power), in descending order of strength.

---

# Henry VIII

## Protestant Ideas
1. **Lollardy, Tyndale, and the English Bible** — the single clearest evidence of genuine, organic popular religious change predating and independent of Henry's own political needs, with Tyndale's 1525 New Testament treated as one of the most consequential single events of the whole Reformation
2. **The Divorce Crisis, Anne Boleyn, and the Break with Rome (Wooding review)** — the Wooding/Guy/Fox material gives the most sophisticated, historiographically rich evidence of genuine personal evangelical conviction (Henry's "deeply held, if idiosyncratic" faith, Anne's "Lefèvrist" evangelicalism) complicating any purely cynical reading
3. **Henry's Final Years: Conservatism Reasserted and the Act of Six Articles (1539 Zurich letter)** — a rare piece of genuine primary-source evidence (private reformist correspondence, not state propaganda) showing real, if cautious, doctrinal movement underway
4. **Henry's Claim to Imperial Authority** — shows the specific biblical/theological argument (kings, not popes, in Scripture) that gave Henry's supremacy its intellectual content, even if adopted for political convenience
5. **Why Henry Did Not Return to Rome** — the "genie of Protestantism" argument: once biblical authority was invoked to justify the break, it could not be controlled or limited afterward
6. **Anticlericalism: The Hunne Case and Simon Fish** — useful as background evidence that English anticlerical sentiment existed independently of Henry's personal crisis, though weaker since it is about anticlericalism rather than Protestant doctrine specifically
7. **Cromwell's Injunctions and the Ten Articles** — shows real, accelerating doctrinal content (Becket shrine, Ten Articles' silence on four sacraments), though framed explicitly as managed and incremental rather than driven by conviction

## Financial Motivation
1. **Henry's Achievement and the Dissolution of the Monasteries** — the strongest, most direct evidence in the whole essay: the post-war historiographical consensus across all schools agrees wealth, not religion, was the "vital factor" behind the dissolution
2. **How Far Was the Dissolution Pre-Planned, and Why So Little Resistance?** — reinforces the financial reading by showing the dissolution was an opportunistic pursuit of available wealth (the 1538 "sweep") rather than ideologically driven
3. **The Amicable Grant and Its Collapse** — concrete evidence of the financial pressures (war costs) that shaped early Tudor policy and provoked serious popular resistance when taxation overreached
4. **Henry's Marriage to Catherine of Aragon and Early Military Glory** — establishes the costly pattern of war taxation that set financial precedent for the reign
5. **The Reign of Henry VII and Tudor Dynasty Foundations** — useful as background/contrast: Henry VII's hoarding versus Henry VIII's profligacy explains the financial pressure building from the very start of the reign

## Power
1. **Convocation and the Royal Supremacy, 1531–1532** — the single clearest constitutional moment of pure power assertion: Henry breaking Magna Carta and his own coronation oath to force through the supremacy
2. **Modern Historiographical Reflection: Henry as Political Operator (Starkey)** — Starkey's "this was totally top down" is one of the most direct, citable single statements available anywhere in the whole essay for the Power argument
3. **The Oath, Divisiveness, and the Trial of Thomas More** — shows the state's willingness to use judicial execution to enforce political conformity to the supremacy
4. **The Fall of Thomas Cromwell** — demonstrates that even the most powerful minister's position depended entirely on personal royal favour rather than institutional security, and shows religious policy entangled with court faction and personal vendetta
5. **The Pilgrimage of Grace** — shows the scale of coercive state power needed (30,000+ rebels eventually crushed through deception and brutal reprisal) to suppress popular resistance to the Reformation
6. **The Secret Marriage and Excommunication** — Henry and Anne defying the Pope, Charles V, and London crowds alike shows raw assertion of will over consensus
7. **Why Henry Did Not Return to Rome** — the Great Bible title page is a powerful visual/symbolic source for absolute royal authority over both church and state
8. **The Turn to Cambridge and Thomas Cranmer** — shows the state's willingness to manufacture intellectual consensus through bribery and coercion rather than genuine persuasion

# Edward VI

## Protestant Ideas
1. **Who Was Responsible for the Edwardian Religious Reformation?** — the most direct, content-rich section: covers the 1549 and 1552 Prayer Books, the rejection of transubstantiation, and the 42 Articles, the clearest unambiguous doctrinal advances anywhere in the whole period
2. **Accession, Education, and the Question of How Much Power Edward Wielded** — strong evidence of genuine top-down personal conviction: Edward's own theological writings (the 1549 treatise against papal supremacy) and his evangelical tutors' formation
3. **Who Designed the "Devise for the Succession"?** — Edward's own forceful personal intervention to override his law officers, and his framing of Jane Grey as his "spiritual sister," is strong evidence of genuine personal religious conviction driving a major political decision
4. **Edward's Conflict with Mary, the 1552 Progress, and Final Illness** — Mary's pointed 1551 quote and the religious dimension of their conflict shows Protestant conviction as a genuinely lived, contested issue at the highest level
5. **The 1549 Rebellions and Somerset's Fall** — opposition to the new Prayer Book as the direct trigger for the western rising is strong evidence Protestant change provoked real popular resistance, cutting both ways as evidence of the ideas' importance

## Financial Motivation
1. **Who Was Responsible for the Edwardian Religious Reformation?** — Jennifer Loach's reading of Somerset's reform enthusiasm as substantially self-interested, plus MacCulloch's point about advisers' self-interest in further chantry wealth, is the most direct financial evidence available
2. **Is It Fair to Call Northumberland the "Bad Duke"?** — the reduction of crown debt from £300,000, the Boulogne sale, and the currency restoration show financial necessity as the dominant practical concern of Edwardian government
3. **Does Somerset Deserve His Reputation as the "Good Duke"?** — coinage debasement and the financial strain of simultaneous wars with Scotland and France shows financial crisis driving and ultimately undermining policy
4. **Conclusion: Assessing the Edwardian Reformation** — MacCulloch's quote on advisers' self-interest in diverting church wealth is a clean, citable single piece of evidence

## Power
1. **The 1549 Rebellions and Somerset's Fall** — the explicit treason charge of "ambition" against Somerset is the most direct possible evidence that power, not religion, was the stated reason for his removal
2. **Is It Fair to Call Northumberland the "Bad Duke"?** — Northumberland's execution of Somerset despite a prior reconciliation gesture, and his entire governing strategy organised around consolidating control, is strong evidence of naked power politics
3. **Who Designed the "Devise for the Succession"?** — Northumberland's marriage of his son to Jane Grey just before the devise was finalised remains the strongest single piece of evidence for reading the whole succession scheme as power-driven
4. **Accession, Education, and the Question of How Much Power Edward Wielded** — Northumberland's calculated, patient cultivation of Edward's trust to control him indirectly is a sophisticated illustration of how power actually operated behind the scenes
5. **Conclusion: Assessing the Edwardian Reformation** — Loades's broader argument about the transformation of English kingship itself, from "the monarch was the kingdom" onward, is significant evidence that institutional power, not religious change, was the era's most durable legacy

# Mary I

## Protestant Ideas
1. **Historiographical Reassessment: Was Mary's Reign a Failure?** — Marshall's argument that Mary's reign permanently entrenched Protestant national identity (via the burnings) and that early Elizabethan bishops faced unprecedented difficulty enforcing Catholic removal is the strongest, most sophisticated evidence of genuine, hardened popular Protestant conviction by this point
2. **The Marian Counter-Reformation** — the persistence of an "entrenched and substantial" Protestant minority specifically in London and the South East directly continues the same regional pattern traced back to Lollardy, reinforcing a real popular Protestant base
3. **Mary's Spanish Marriage, the Loss of Calais, and Her Death** — the way the burnings, Spanish courtiers, and Philip's unpopularity fuelled Protestant propaganda shows Protestant identity being actively reinforced through opposition to Mary's policies

## Financial Motivation
1. **The Marian Counter-Reformation** — only very thin evidence exists for this reign; the section explicitly notes no significant new financial detail beyond the continuity of previously confiscated Church lands remaining in lay hands
*(Note: Mary's reign is the weakest of the four monarchs for the Financial Motivation factor — there is no rich case study here comparable to the Henrician dissolution material.)*

## Power
1. **The Marian Counter-Reformation** — the direct contrast between Mary's "handicapping" use of coercive persecution and its demonstrable failure to achieve religious uniformity is the strongest single case study anywhere in the whole essay for showing the limits of power as a tool of religious change
2. **Historiographical Reassessment: Was Mary's Reign a Failure?** — Duffy's Fires of Faith thesis, that the persecution was "to a considerable extent successful," directly complicates any simple reading of the burnings as pure policy failure, making this the most sophisticated treatment of power's effectiveness anywhere in the sources
3. **Mary's Spanish Marriage, the Loss of Calais, and Her Death** — the loss of Calais and Wyatt's rebellion show the practical limits of royal power once foreign policy and unpopular marriage choices compounded domestic religious unpopularity

# Elizabeth I

## Power
1. **The Role of Longevity and Legacy** — the most direct, explicit evidence anywhere in the entire essay that political contingency (sheer length of reign), not the inherent strength of Protestant ideas, secured the Reformation's ultimate success; the explicit "had she died of smallpox in 1562" counterfactual is a uniquely strong citable point
2. **Historiographical Reassessment: Was Elizabethan "Anglicanism" a Myth?** — the Jones/Hudson "twin death blows" research and Marshall's framing show even the most apparently consensus-driven moment of the period was in fact a decisive, one-sided use of state and parliamentary power
3. **Enforcement, Foreign Threat, and the Suppression of Catholicism** — the 1563 Act of Uniformity and escalating Treason Laws show direct, sustained use of coercive state power to eliminate Catholicism as a political force
4. **The Religious Settlement of 1559** — the settlement's deliberate framing as political compromise, with enforcement consistently pushing further in a Protestant direction than the formal text required, shows power operating both explicitly and through quiet, unannounced practical implementation

## Protestant Ideas
1. **Historiographical Reassessment: Was Elizabethan "Anglicanism" a Myth?** — the Jones/Hudson finding that the 1559 settlement was unambiguously Protestant in intent from the outset, with opposition coming from Catholics rather than Puritans, is the strongest and most historiographically current evidence for reading Elizabeth's reign as straightforwardly Protestant
2. **The Religious Settlement of 1559** — the settlement's substantive content (reissuing Cranmer's 1552 Prayer Book and 1553 Articles almost unchanged) shows continuity with the most radical Edwardian Protestant theology beneath a more conciliatory surface
3. **The Role of Longevity and Legacy** — the "common religion and a common enemy" framing, linking back ironically to Henry's own Fidei Defensor title, shows Protestantism and English national identity becoming genuinely fused by the end of the period
