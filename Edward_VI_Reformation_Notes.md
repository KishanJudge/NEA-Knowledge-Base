# Edward VI (1547–1553): Case Studies by Factor

---

# Protestant Ideas

### Edward's Evangelical Education and Personal Conviction
The strongest evidence that Protestant ideas drove the Edwardian Reformation from the top: Edward's own writings show genuine theological conviction, not merely performed piety imposed by advisers.
- Edward was tutored by committed Cambridge-educated evangelicals: Richard Cox (arrogant and dogmatic, did not spare the birch on his princely pupil), Roger Ascham, and John Cheke — all of whom demonstrated zealous commitment to radical Protestant reform and instilled in Edward both Protestant conviction and hatred of the Pope
- Cox beat Edward specifically to overcome his "captain will" — suggesting Edward's Protestant formation was contested as well as enthusiastic, not simply passive absorption
- Surviving school essays show direct influence of tutors' anti-papal Protestant conviction; specific example: Edward's French-language treatise against papal supremacy, in which his tutor Jean Belmain repeatedly crossed out Edward's use of "we" to refer to the Protestant faithful and replaced it with "they" — "more impersonal, more royal"; Edward dutifully incorporated the change into the next draft but the narrator notes this "rather went against the grain," since Edward would later prove "prepared to be just as radical in practice as he'd been on paper"
- MacCulloch draws attention to the "zeal and theological competence" of Edward's surviving religious writings — including a remarkable treatise against papal supremacy written in 1549, when he was eleven — distinguishing genuine theological engagement from merely performed piety
- Edward's Chronicle never mentions sermons (Jennifer Loach: this "casts doubt on the depth of his supposed zeal") but it is known he kept a separate, now lost, ledger of court sermon notes
- Acts consistent with personal Protestant conviction: repeatedly rebuking Mary for her adherence to the Catholic Mass; insisting on removal of references to the saints from the oath of supremacy; showing complete lack of sympathy for the western rebels who burned the 1549 Prayer Book, instead treating the suppression as a series of military exercises and recording with apparent excitement that "900 of the rebels were massacred in a single day"
- Catherine Davies: "chivalry and piety are not mutually exclusive" — Edward's love of jousting, hunting, and military exercise did not preclude genuine religious seriousness

### The Edwardian Reformation in Practice: Churches Stripped and the Prayer Books
The most direct, visible evidence of Protestant ideas being implemented at state level — the physical transformation of English church life between 1547 and 1553.
- Repeal of the Act of Six Articles almost immediately on Edward's accession reversed Henry's final Catholic reassertion
- From the earliest months of the reign churches were whitewashed and stripped of images; stained glass smashed, altars removed, candles extinguished, the rood (image of the suffering Christ, his grieving mother the Virgin, and St John the apostle) torn down, burned, and replaced by Edward's royal arms
- Some radical parishes in London began destroying Catholic imagery themselves before the government made it official — the government then formalised what had already started from below, to harness the movement and maintain public order
- The first Book of Common Prayer (March 1549) introduced English as the language of worship for the first time — described as not particularly radical in theology but revolutionary in language
- The second Book of Common Prayer (1552), written by Cranmer and described as Edward's "most decisive move towards truly Protestant England": the two crosses that the priest made during the 1549 communion service — which allowed people to believe the Catholic miracle of transubstantiation was still occurring — were removed entirely; direct quote of the new 1552 wording: "Take and eat this in remembrance that Christ died for thee, and feed on him in thy heart by faith, with thanksgiving"; narrator's assessment: "a whole world of Catholic religiosity is dead, and between the covers of this book, a new Protestant England, Edward's England, is born"
- June 1553: the 42 Articles issued, formally enshrining the Protestant doctrine of justification by faith and denying the Catholic doctrine of purgatory; modified as the 39 Articles in 1563, these remained doctrinally central to the Church of England throughout Elizabeth's reign and beyond
- The dissolution of the chantries — endowments for prayers for souls in purgatory — was theologically consistent with Protestant rejection of purgatory; some chantries were converted into grammar schools named after Edward VI, hence the large number still bearing his name today
- Imposition of clerical celibacy was ended; clergy permitted to marry — a specifically Protestant break from Catholic practice
- G.R. Elton's verdict: by 1553 "England was almost certainly nearer to being a Protestant country than to anything else" — the clearest single statement of the Protestant-ideas argument applied to Edward's reign
- Eamon Duffy, by contrast, emphasises resistance to change and the persistence of old ways, supported by local studies finding evangelical Protestants in a tiny minority outside London and the Southeast

### Cranmer's Role as the Theological Architect
Cranmer is the clearest single individual embodiment of Protestant ideas driving the Edwardian Reformation — his conviction preceded and outlasted the political contexts of any individual reign.
- Cranmer had forged strong personal links with leading continental reformers, particularly Martin Bucer at Strasbourg; from the very beginning of Edward's reign he sought to push England to the forefront of the international Protestant Reformation
- At Edward's coronation, Cranmer delivered a sermon deliberately undermining the traditional meaning of the service: he told the congregation that the Church's anointing and crowning "could add nothing to Edward's intrinsic power"; instead Edward's power came directly from God, given for a single purpose — to reform and purify the Church of England; narrator: "this was a sustained attack on the Church of England as then constituted" and "a fitting start to what was to be a radical reign"
- Cranmer described as "the driving force behind" all the major religious changes of the reign; the Cranmer/Somerset/Northumberland working relationship is described as Cranmer providing the theological direction ("the thought") while Somerset and Northumberland provided the political machinery ("the police")
- His comparison of Edward to the Old Testament Josiah — who purged his land of idols — frames the entire reign within an explicitly Protestant theological narrative of scriptural reform
- The 1552 Prayer Book is described as Cranmer's greatest achievement and as the single greatest step toward achieving a Protestant England; it remains, with Elizabethan amendments, at the heart of Anglican liturgy today — making Cranmer's Edwardian Protestant ideas one of the most durably influential acts of the entire Reformation period

### Edward's Personal Religious Conflict with Mary
Edward's sustained personal attempt to prevent his Catholic half-sister from continuing to hear Mass shows Protestant conviction operating at the highest level of government as a genuinely held personal position, not merely a political calculation.
- Mary was in her thirties, unmarried, often ill, but could not be ignored as heir to the throne and a powerful figurehead for Catholic resistance
- The 1549 Prayer Book specifically — the document that had already provoked a western rebellion — was the point of conflict between them: Mary refused to follow its requirements and continued hearing the illegal Latin Mass
- Spring 1550: Charles V's ambassadors formally requested that Mary and her household be exempted from the new religious legislation; Edward's own mind was "closed" and he refused the request
- Mary subsequently asked Charles to help her flee to Catholic Europe; Edward responded by sending troops to cut her off by land and ships to intercept the imperial fleet; it was ultimately Mary's own hesitations, as much as Edward's countermeasures, that led her to abandon the escape plan
- Mary's specific response to Edward's 1551 instruction to follow his religious reforms, when he was thirteen and she was thirty-five: "when the king's Majesty... shall come to such years that he may be able to judge things himself, his Majesty shall find me ready to obey his orders in religion: but now in these years, although the good sweet king have more knowledge than any other of his years, yet is it not possible that he can be a judge in these things" — a direct assertion that Edward was too young to have genuine religious authority, challenging his conviction as well as his power
- Edward ordered Mary's chaplains taken away from her; several were imprisoned in the Tower; Mary continued to hear Mass regardless
- The final observation: "Mary herself was safe. She was Edward's legal heir. At the end, Edward could not touch her" — showing the limits of even genuine Protestant conviction when constrained by the fixed rules of dynastic succession

# Financial Motivation

### The Dissolution of the Chantries and Redistribution of Church Wealth
The Edwardian dissolution of the chantries follows directly from the logic of Henry's dissolution of the monasteries — Protestant theological arguments provided justification for a programme that also served clear financial interests.
- Since chantries were endowments to fund prayers for souls in purgatory, and purgatory was a doctrine Protestantism explicitly rejected, their abolition had a ready theological justification; in practice, chantry chapels had often accumulated enormous wealth
- Their dissolution transferred this wealth directly to the Crown and to local elites who purchased former chantry properties
- Jennifer Loach's assessment of Somerset's interest in reform: she sees it as "mainly motivated by the chance to consolidate the royal supremacy and his own personal assets, which swelled considerably with the dissolution of church property" — a direct argument that financial self-interest, not doctrinal conviction, was Somerset's primary motivation for supporting Protestant reform
- MacCulloch's observation that Edward's advisers "could not have failed to notice that a programme of religious change also gave the possibility of diverting some of the old Church's huge wealth to their own pockets" — reinforcing the financial reading at an even earlier stage of the reign
- Some former chantries were converted into schools (the Edward VI grammar schools), but the majority of chantry wealth passed to secular hands; this continued the pattern established by the Henrician dissolution of creating a propertied class with a vested financial interest in the continuation of a Protestant settlement

### Somerset's Financial Mismanagement and Its Consequences
The financial crisis of Somerset's Protectorate shows financial motivation operating as a negative force — the pursuit of costly military ambitions destabilised the entire government and created the conditions for the rebellions that ended his rule.
- Somerset inherited from Henry a financially precarious crown and proceeded to pursue simultaneous wars on two fronts: Scotland and France
- He relied heavily on expensive mercenaries; twice debased the coinage (melting it down and reissuing with a lower silver content to raise quick cash), creating widespread distrust and fuelling inflation; taxed sheep; sold ecclesiastical property
- By September 1549 the crown was nearly bankrupt despite these measures
- Somerset's 33-month Protectorate saw 76 proclamations — more than in any comparable period of the century — partly as a way of governing without the expense and political difficulty of full parliamentary sessions
- By 1550, under Northumberland, the crown debt had reached £300,000 and inflation had risen approximately 75% in two years — a direct consequence of Somerset's financial recklessness
- Edward's 1552 Progress reveals the depth of the crisis at personal level: the royal cash reserves were carried in just a couple of small coffers or chests — "small specifically because the incompetence and corruption of Edward's council had nearly destroyed the royal finances"; Edward was distressed and personally humiliated that his former stepmother Anne of Cleves had been told she could not have her allowance paid, since he had "a very powerful sense of honour"

### Northumberland's Financial Stabilisation
Northumberland's success in restoring crown finances shows financial necessity, not Protestant conviction, was the dominant concern of the second half of Edward's reign.
- Northumberland inherited from Somerset a crown debt of £300,000 and 75% inflation in two years, with war continuing against both France and Scotland
- He immediately dropped Somerset's expensive Scottish war and negotiated peace with France, including surrendering Boulogne (triumphantly acquired by Henry VIII in 1544 at great cost) — A.F. Pollard called this Treaty of Boulogne "the most ignominious" signed by England in the century, though Edward's own Chronicle records general public rejoicing at the peace
- Northumberland attempted to restore the coinage after multiple debasements under Henry and Somerset; he announced his intention in advance, which triggered a near-complete fiscal breakdown in May 1552; the situation was rescued by Sir Thomas Gresham (appointed by Northumberland as the king's agent on the Antwerp exchange — Gresham also founded the Royal Exchange and Gresham College), whose profitable activities helped stabilise the currency
- By the end of 1553, almost all of the crown's debt had been repaid — described as "an incredible achievement"
- Barrett L. Beer's assessment of Northumberland's governing priority: he "personally supplied few of the ideas" but "lent his authority and gave encouragement to the experts," pursuing a clear strategy to "reduce the king's debts, to consolidate his position as supreme head of the church, and to reinforce the privy council's administrative machinery" — financial stabilisation listed first among his objectives

# Power

### Somerset's Seizure of the Protectorate
Somerset's subversion of Henry's will before the old king was even buried shows power, not Protestant conviction, was the primary concern of the men who actually governed England in Edward's name from the outset.
- Henry VIII's will specified a collective regency council to govern until Edward's majority; even before Henry's death Somerset had plotted with Henry's secretary Sir William Paget to subvert this arrangement
- He secured the other executors' support by promising titles, commissions, and lands; they appointed him Lord Protector of the Realm and Governor of the King's Person
- A patent of 12 March 1547 widened his remit to allow him to act alone — giving him more power than any other subject since the beginning of Tudor rule
- Somerset ruled as a "quasi-king," largely ignoring the young king except for ceremonial purposes; he did not even allow Edward pocket money — a deliberate strategy to control the king's personal relationships and prevent rival influence
- The 76 proclamations he issued over 33 months, combined with his habit of being recorded as attending Privy Council meetings he actually missed (his signature added to documents later), show a minister ruling through personal authority rather than institutional process
- Dale Hoak's verdict: his "near-abandonment" of the council brought the government "close to ruin"
- Thomas Seymour's rival bid for power — secretly bribing Edward with pocket money, telling him "Ye are a beggarly king, ye have no money to play or to give," attempting to marry Princess Elizabeth, eventually attempting to abduct the king (killed the king's spaniel in the process and was arrested sword in hand) — shows power competition at the most personal and dangerous level; Somerset had his own brother attainted and executed in January 1549, declaring "how sorowfull a case this was unto hym" — G.W. Bernard's verdict: no "fratricidal bitterness" was involved, Somerset had to regard his duty to the king over family loyalty

### The 1549 Rebellions and Somerset's Fall
The most concentrated case study of power politics in the reign: Somerset was removed not for religious failure but for incompetence, autocratic rule, and the specific charge of personal ambition — showing power, not Protestant ideas, determined who governed England.
- April to August 1549: domestic unrest in approximately fifteen counties — described as the century's "greatest crisis" (Dale Hoak); largest risings in the southwest (western rising against the Prayer Book) and East Anglia (Kett's rebellion near Norwich)
- Robert Kett himself was a prosperous tanner, not a peasant revolutionary: Andy Wood's proto-Marxist reading of the rebellion as "commoner insurgency driven by proto-Marxist resistance to agrarian capitalism" and part of "the long-term formation of the working class" is challenged by Jennifer Loach's demonstration that many rebel leaders ranked just below the gentry; Kett formed an elected council who sent their demands to the king
- Somerset slow to respond; MacCulloch attributes this to sympathy with the rebels' grievances, Barrett L. Beer to poor communication; eventually Somerset called on John Dudley to suppress Kett's rebellion; Dudley killed at least 2,000 rebels and returned to London without disbanding his troops — a calculated political move
- Somerset dragged Edward out of bed on the night of 6 October 1549 and took him by night ride to Windsor Castle; direct Chronicle quote: "That night, with all the people, at nine or ten o'clock at night, I went to Windsor and there was watch and ward kept every night"
- Edward's response: outraged, he exaggerated his cold symptoms and complained vociferously about the quality of his accommodation — no galleries or gardens to walk in; this was a deliberate signal that royal favour (on which any minister's power depended even during a minority) had been withdrawn
- Somerset confessed to 29 counts of treason; Edward's Chronicle lists the official charges: "false, ambition, vain glory, entering into rash wars, enriching himself of my treasure, following his own opinion and doing all by his own authority" — Edward considered the last of these ("doing all by his own authority") "the real nub of the matter"; one charge was specifically "ambition" — trying to increase his own personal power
- Northumberland did not formally become Protector; in February 1550 he secured Edward's approval for appointment as Great Master and Lord President — control of the king's household and supervision of the council

### Northumberland's Management of Edward and the Privy Council
Northumberland's subtle, sophisticated approach to governing through rather than around the king illustrates how real power operated behind the formal constitutional structure throughout the reign.
- A.F. Pollard labelled Northumberland the "subtlest intriguer in history"; the evidence against this is that he appears to have been a genuinely trusted ally of Somerset until the circumstances of 1549 forced his hand — he played a distinguished role at the Battle of Pinkie and received £100-worth of land from Somerset in gratitude
- Northumberland knew Edward would soon reach his majority and risked losing influence if his actions were out of tune with the king's wishes; from August 1551 he ensured Edward regularly attended council meetings; from May 1552 the king was directly participating in financial affairs
- Dale Hoak's key observation: Northumberland could "afford to allow Edward's exercise of power in more peripheral matters, giving the impression that he was governing 'as a king counselled' while in reality much of the king's thinking was being done for him"
- A French visitor documented Northumberland's technique explicitly: "he visited the King secretly at night in the King's chamber... The next day the young Prince came to his council and proposed matters as if they were his own; consequently, everyone was amazed"
- Northumberland restored genuine conciliar government: while Somerset had increasingly circumvented his council, Northumberland created one strong enough to take decisions even in his absence but which he could still control — Dale Hoak calls this "Northumberland's main service to the crown," which made the council a principal instrument of monarchical government retained to the end of the Tudor period
- He pursued reconciliation: ordered Somerset's release from prison in February 1550, readmitted him to the Privy Council in April, married one of his sons to Somerset's daughter Anne in a public gesture; Somerset continued to intrigue against him, leading Northumberland to have him executed for felony on 22 January 1552 — convicted not of treason (Somerset successfully argued his way out of this charge) but of felony for assembling an illegal force, of which he was probably guilty

### The Devise for the Succession
The succession crisis of 1553 shows power and Protestant ideas so entangled as to be inseparable — but with power (specifically the fear of a Catholic queen) providing the structural logic and Protestant conviction providing the personal motivation.
- The traditional view: Northumberland bullied the dying Edward into diverting the succession to Lady Jane Grey to protect his own power; supporting evidence — Jane married Northumberland's son Guildford on 21 May 1553, just before the devise was finalised
- The counter-evidence: Jane had not been Northumberland's first choice of bride for his son; when Edward's law officers demurred at breaching Henry's statute of succession, it was Edward himself "with sharp words and angry countenance" who commanded them to obey
- The devise itself, written in the "unformed hand of a 15-year-old boy" but with, as the Starkey documentary puts it, "the mind entirely adult and mature": Edward excluded Mary (Catholic) and then Elizabeth (because she would not leapfrog Mary in the succession order), passing the crown to descendants of his aunt Mary — initially to their "heirs male" (i.e. future sons), but when Edward realised he was dying and there was no time for sons to be born, he altered the devise in his own handwriting: "the Lady Jane's heirs male" became, by removing an "s" and adding "and her" above a caret mark, "the Lady Jane and her heirs male" — three strokes of the pen
- Edward regarded Jane as his "spiritual sister" who had absorbed the same evangelical Protestant formation as himself and could be trusted to carry his reformation forward — a specifically religious motivation
- Edward personally "bullied and cajoled" Privy Councillors into endorsing the devise in his final days
- His death was kept secret even from his half-sisters — "there were no last farewells, for nothing must get in the way of his scheme to save Protestant England"; his last words were a prayer in English of his own devising
- The scheme failed immediately: the English people declared for Mary, preferring a legitimate Tudor heir over a Protestant candidate; this rejection of Jane in favour of a committed Catholic demonstrates that Protestant ideas had not yet secured genuinely broad popular loyalty — the key counter-argument to G.R. Elton's claim that England was "almost certainly nearer to being a Protestant country than to anything else" by 1553

### Edward's 1552 Progress and His Vision for England
Edward's own articulation of his ambitions for England shows a vision driven simultaneously by Protestant conviction and by recognisably power-driven concerns — military, economic, and administrative modernisation.
- Summer 1552, accompanied by nearly 4,000 mounted men (most sent back to London quickly as too many to feed), Edward made his first real tour of his kingdom — his first chance to "inspect England, his England"
- Financially humiliating: royal cash reserves were carried in just a couple of small coffers; Anne of Cleves had been told she could not have her allowance paid; Edward had a "very powerful sense of honour" and found the near-bankruptcy personally humiliating compared to his father's wealth
- At Portsmouth, Edward inspected Henry's coastal fortifications with "a cool and expert eye": praised the harbour as "superb, a mile long, and able to take the greatest ships in Christendom"; judged the two forts protecting the Camber entrance as "ill-built, old-fashioned, and wrongly sited" and ordered them reconstructed immediately; back in London, item one on his government action list was the fortification of Portsmouth
- Edward's wider ambitions for England, had he lived: he would have militarised the country with a vast standing army and network of efficient fortifications; begun centralising the economy, planning markets to rival Antwerp and investing in industry in ways not seen in Europe for at least a century; created "an England of extreme religion — a puritanical religion rigorously enforced and policed"; the Starkey documentary's explicit comparison: "a modern Protestant state, more akin to what happened in Prussia or Sweden than anything we now associate with England"
- His final illness began in February 1553 (first coughing); by March he looked "very weak and thin"; by April he was vomiting blood and "black and greenish matter"; modern diagnosis: a suppurating pulmonary infection; he died 6 July 1553
- The counterfactual assessment is directly relevant to the essay: had Edward lived, Protestant ideas would have been implemented with a thoroughness and rigour that went far beyond anything achieved under Henry, Edward, or Elizabeth — suggesting that what actually happened was a much more compromised, incomplete version of Protestant reform than its most committed advocates intended
