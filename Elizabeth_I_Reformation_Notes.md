# Elizabeth I (1558–1603): Case Studies by Factor

---

# Protestant Ideas

### Elizabeth's Personal Religious Beliefs
The question of whether Elizabeth was a sincere Protestant or a political pragmatist is fundamental to assessing whether Protestant ideas drove her Reformation — historians have disagreed sharply on this.
- Protestant Bishop John Hooper wrote to the Swiss reformer Bullinger in February 1550 that Elizabeth "not only knows what the true religion is, but has acquired such proficiency in Greek and Latin that she is able to defend it"
- Historian A. F. Pollard (1919) claimed Elizabeth was "indifferent to religion"; Sir John Neale (1950s) argued she planned to return England to the "National Catholicism" of her father's day
- The now widely accepted view, stated explicitly by Christopher Haigh in his 1988 biography, is that "there can be little doubt of Elizabeth's genuine Protestantism"
- Her mother Anne Boleyn's political faction had Lutheran sympathies; her education under Roger Ascham and in the households of Sir Anthony Denny and Queen Catherine Parr was grounded in the "new learning" — the reformist, humanist Protestantism of the mid-sixteenth century
- Winthrop Hudson argues Elizabeth favoured specifically Zwinglian belief — the most scripturally austere form of Protestantism, which treated communion as purely commemorative — as indicated in the wording of the 1559 Book of Common Prayer
- Evidence of genuine personal conviction distinct from political calculation: throughout her reign she had a deep dislike of clerical marriage (a specifically Protestant innovation she personally opposed despite permitting it); she insisted on the use of a cross and candlesticks on the altar of the Royal Chapel (a more conservative, ceremonially inclined Protestant preference that displeased Puritans); Patrick Collinson notes the Elizabethan compromise "was a concession not only to the conservative prejudices of Elizabeth's subjects, but to her own feelings"
- Elizabeth is famously reputed to have said "I will not make a window into men's souls" — though no written record of this exact phrase exists, it accurately captures her stated policy of tolerating private Catholic belief while requiring outward conformity to the settlement

### The Elizabethan Church Settlement, 1559–1563
The settlement is the most direct evidence of Protestant ideas shaping the Reformation under Elizabeth, but is simultaneously contested as evidence of power and financial motivation — making it the most versatile and complex case study of her reign.
- The Acts of Supremacy and Uniformity were passed by Parliament in May 1559; the Royal Injunctions followed in July 1559; the Thirty Nine Articles were produced by Convocation in 1563 and given parliamentary force by the Subscription Act of 1571
- Act of Supremacy: Elizabeth became "Supreme Governor" rather than "Supreme Head" — wording chosen to placate both Catholics (who disliked a female church head) and more extreme Protestants; required all clergy to swear an oath recognising her authority; only 4% of lower clergy ever refused, around 200 in total in the period November 1559 to November 1564
- Act of Uniformity: reintroduced church attendance on Sundays and Holy Days under a 12d fine; the new Book of Common Prayer was based on the Edwardian Books of 1549 and 1552, with wording deliberately designed to be acceptable to Catholics (who believed in the real presence of Christ at communion), Lutherans (who believed in consubstantiation — a spiritual presence), and Zwinglians (who regarded communion as purely commemorative)
- Royal Injunctions (57 rules): closed shrines and outlawed pilgrimage (anti-Catholic); required English Bibles in all churches; required licenced preaching; but used wafers instead of bread at communion (Catholic practice), permitted kneeling at communion (associated with the real presence), and prevented further destruction of altars — all disappointing to radical Protestants
- Thirty Nine Articles (1563/1571): Susan Doran describes them as "something of a hybrid, containing features that were Lutheran, Zwinglian and Calvinist"; Article XVII on predestination was worded to avoid full Calvinist commitment while remaining acceptable to Lutherans and Zwinglians; Article XXVIII denied the real presence of Christ in the communion service — a clear rejection of Catholic transubstantiation
- Norman Jones, in Faith by Statute (1982), demonstrated that the settlement was challenged from the Catholic Lords, not by radical Protestants in the Commons — overturning Neale's earlier claim that a "Puritan choir" in the Commons had forced Elizabeth toward a more Protestant settlement than she wanted; only after imprisoning some Catholic Bishops did the Act of Uniformity pass the Lords by just three votes
- Elizabeth allowed Convocation rather than Parliament to produce the Thirty Nine Articles, which suggests she regarded their doctrinal content as genuinely a matter of religious conviction rather than purely of constitutional control
- The Elizabethan settlement is described by Haigh (1988) as one whose "decisions seemed to make no coherent sense, and the 'Elizabethan Settlement' had, apparently, settled nothing; but to the surprise of everyone except the Queen, the uneasy compromise lasted"

### The Rise and Decline of Puritanism
The Puritan challenge to Elizabeth's settlement is simultaneously evidence that Protestant ideas did influence the Reformation's development (Puritans forced real compromises) and evidence that the settlement was not principally driven by Protestant ideas (Puritans' complaints show it had not gone far enough for committed Protestants).
- The Vestiarian Controversy peaked in 1566 when several clergy refused to obey rules on clerical dress in the Act of Uniformity, arguing the specified vestments were Catholic and therefore superstitious; Archbishop Matthew Parker issued his Book of Advertisements to enforce conformity; this split between the settlement and more committed Protestants shows the settlement's Protestant credentials were contested from the outset
- Thomas Cartwright's spring lectures at Cambridge 1570 openly criticised the structure of the Elizabethan Church (archbishops, bishops, parsons, deacons) and proposed a Presbyterian model of independent congregations led by lay elders — drawn from Geneva under Calvin and Scotland under Knox; he was removed from his Cambridge professorship in December 1570 by John Whitgift
- John Field and Thomas Wilcox published the Admonition to Parliament in 1572, calling for radical reform of Church government and practice toward a Calvinist model; Field received one year's imprisonment; the movement had aristocratic support from the Earl of Leicester and Sir Francis Walsingham
- Parliamentary attempts to change the settlement failed in 1571 (Strickland imprisoned), 1572, 1584, 1585, and 1587 (Anthony Cope and four other MPs imprisoned in the Tower) — each time blocked by Elizabeth and the Privy Council
- Whitgift (Archbishop from 1583) issued Three Articles requiring clergy to acknowledge royal supremacy, accept the Prayer Book, and affirm the Thirty Nine Articles conformed to the word of God; this created a crisis of conscience for many clergy; Whitgift was forced to back down and compromise, showing Protestant arguments had real force
- Martin Marprelate Tracts (seven in all, published 1588-1589) were an extreme anonymous attack on Church structure; even Cartwright was shocked; Whitgift used the Courts of High Commission and Star Chamber to break the Puritan press
- Decline of Presbyterianism: John Field died 1588; Earl of Leicester died 1588; Sir Francis Walsingham died 1590; the defeat of the Spanish Armada in 1588 reduced the perceived Catholic threat that had made Puritanism politically attractive; by 1593 the Presbyterian movement was "effectively leaderless and clearly in decline"
- The Lambeth Articles of 1595 — nine points of doctrine approved by Whitgift — largely reasserted the fundamentally Calvinist theological character of the Elizabethan Church, showing that beneath its ceremonial compromises, the settlement's doctrinal core remained Protestant; this proved acceptable to both Puritans and moderates
- Two new Cambridge colleges were established during Elizabeth's reign (Sidney Sussex and Emmanuel) specifically to train Protestant clergy; by 1603 the Church had made moves toward providing an all-graduate Protestant clergy
- The Presbyterian movement comprised around 300 clergy and some 75,000 lay members by 1603 — reduced to a Protestant fringe, but still alive, and Puritan demands would re-emerge as a major political crisis under James I and Charles I, suggesting Elizabeth's failure to accommodate the Protestant left created the very problem she had tried to prevent

### The Survival of Catholicism and the Catholic Threat
The persistence of Catholicism throughout the reign demonstrates both the limits of what Protestant ideas could achieve and — through the escalating response it provoked — the extent to which the Protestant settlement was genuinely under threat throughout the period.
- At the beginning of the reign most people in England were still Catholic; Eamon Duffy's study of wills at the beginning of Elizabeth's reign (The Stripping of the Altars) showed Catholic beliefs were held by "large sections of the population"
- The first decade (1558-68) saw little persecution of Catholics; Elizabeth is quoted (reputed) as saying "I will not make a window into men's souls"; fines for recusancy were rarely demanded; Catholics survived largely by outwardly attending Anglican services
- Northern Rebellion 1569: the rebel earls entered Durham Cathedral on 14 November 1569, tore up Protestant Prayer Books, celebrated the Catholic Mass, and carried a banner displaying the Five Wounds of Christ (the same emblem as the Pilgrimage of Grace 1536); Anthony Fletcher and Diarmaid MacCulloch identify strong Catholic influence, with Richard Norton having participated in the Pilgrimage of Grace 33 years before; Lord Dacre raised a further 3,000 troops in January 1570 before defeat at the Battle of Naworth near Carlisle; around 450 of the 700 rebels Elizabeth ordered executed actually suffered that fate
- Pope Pius V excommunicated Elizabeth on 22 February 1570 (Papal Bull Regnans in Excelsis), releasing all Catholics from allegiance to her — but this came after the Northern Rebellion had already collapsed
- Catholic missionary effort intensified: from 1574, seminary priests trained at Cardinal William Allen's college at Douai in the Netherlands began arriving in England; 179 arrived in the period 1580-1585 alone; by 1580 100 were already operating in England; from 1580, Jesuits (led initially by Edmund Campion and Robert Parsons) began a mission to re-Catholicise England; Campion was captured and executed for treason December 1581
- By 1603 the Catholic community was served by around 400 seminary priests and 12 Jesuits in England; Catholicism remained particularly strong in Lancashire; 180 Catholics were executed between 1581 and 1603, 120 of them priests; 123 of these were convicted under the 1585 Act against Jesuits and Seminary Priests
- Archpriest Controversy (from 1594): a split emerged between seminary priests (the "Appellants," who wanted Catholicism to be a tolerated religious minority and were prepared to recognise Elizabeth as Queen) and the Jesuits (who wanted a full restoration of Catholicism in line with continental Counter-Reformation); in Elizabeth's final year, 13 Appellants signed a declaration recognising Elizabeth as Queen and refusing to support Catholic invasion
- Alan Dures's conclusion (English Catholicism 1558-1642, 1984): "by 1603 the rigours of Elizabethan government policy had eliminated Catholicism within the Elizabethan church, so that Catholicism was now a distinctive, separated religion"

# Financial Motivation

### The Financial Dimensions of the 1559 Settlement
The clearest and most direct evidence of financial motivation in Elizabeth's reign — the settlement explicitly included mechanisms to extract revenue from the Church, continuing the pattern established by Henry VIII.
- When Elizabeth became Queen the monarchy was in deep financial crisis, mainly due to the French war inherited from Mary; the Crown needed revenue urgently and without increasing taxes on the laity
- Count Feria to Philip II, 19 March 1559 (primary source): "Queen Elizabeth... said that so much money was taken out of the country for the Pope every year that she must put an end to it"
- First Fruits and Tenths Act (1559): brought the Church's most significant tax directly under Crown control — clergy had to pay all of their first year's income and 10% of every subsequent year's income to the monarch; Henry VIII had first taken this tax from the Church; Mary I had returned it to the Church; Elizabeth immediately reclaimed it, providing the Crown with much-needed revenue without increasing lay taxation
- Act of Exchange (1559): gave Elizabeth the right to take over property previously held by bishops; also prevented bishops from renting out land for more than 21 years (except to Elizabeth herself); the result was to transfer considerable wealth from the Church to the Crown, and gave the government the ability to put pressure on bishops by threatening to use parts of the Act against them — ensuring bishops followed Elizabeth's wishes in religious affairs
- Murphy et al. (Britain 1558-1689, 2002): "When Elizabeth I became Queen, the monarchy was in deep financial crisis. This was mainly as a result of the French War. Like her father, Elizabeth used her religious settlement to provide the Crown with much needed revenue"
- Penry Williams (The Later Tudors, 1547-1603): "by about 1580 the established Church had won control of the commanding heights of society" — but Elizabeth's financial exploitation of the Church, through the Act of Exchange and similar measures, was simultaneously "undermining the authority of her bishops"

### The Cost of War with Spain and Ireland
The enormous financial costs of the wars of Elizabeth's later reign show how religious policy became entangled with financial reality, with military expenditure constraining her room for manoeuvre throughout.
- In 1585 Elizabeth signed the Treaty of Nonsuch, officially committing England to supporting the Protestant Dutch rebellion against Spain — at enormous cost
- The Spanish Armada of 1588 was defeated; Elizabeth subsequently sent Drake with 130 ships and 13,600 troops in 1589 to destroy remaining Spanish ships — this failed; privateers attacked Spanish settlements in the Americas; an expedition to Cadiz in 1596 captured the city and destroyed many ships; two more Spanish armadas in 1596-97 were dispersed by storms
- Ireland: from 1595 the major revolt of Hugh O'Neill, Earl of Tyrone, in Ulster drew in 3,500 Spanish troops and cost England enormous resources; a full-scale conquest of Ireland was necessary; the Irish conflict cost Elizabeth approximately the same as the entire war with Spain — estimated at £1.9 billion in modern terms (around £6 trillion in today's money)
- The strain of these financial costs is directly relevant to the essay because England's Protestant settlement was partly sustained by its foreign policy conflicts: the wars with Catholic Spain and the threat of Catholic France meant that religious and financial imperatives pulled in the same direction, making it impossible to disentangle Protestant commitment from sheer financial and strategic self-interest

# Power

### The Act of Supremacy and Elizabeth's Control of the Church
The clearest single demonstration of power motivating the Elizabethan Reformation — restoring royal authority over the Church was the first and most fundamental act of her reign.
- The central aim of the 1559 Act of Supremacy was, in historian Claire Cross's words, to "re-establish formally the Queen's authority over the English Church" after Mary's return of that authority to Rome
- Elizabeth was titled "Supreme Governor" not "Supreme Head" — the formulation was chosen to deflect objections from Catholics (who denied a woman could be head of the Church) and more extreme Protestants (who thought the title inappropriate for a layperson), but the practical effect was identical: full royal control
- The Act required all clergy to take an oath recognising this authority; those who refused were removed, allowing Elizabeth to appoint 25 new bishops who would support her settlement
- It established the Court of High Commission — a commission of clergy and laymen empowered to locate and prosecute Catholic sympathisers — the equivalent of a national religious inspection and enforcement body reporting to the Crown
- The first nationwide visitation began at the end of June 1559 to administer the Oath of Supremacy and deliver the new Royal Injunctions, implementing royal authority across every diocese and parish simultaneously
- Elizabeth consistently opposed any attempt by Parliament to debate or modify the Church Settlement throughout her reign — demonstrating she regarded control of the Church as belonging exclusively to the monarch, not to Parliament: "Queen Elizabeth I believed that the monarch alone had control. She merely used Parliament to introduce these changes"
- When opposition to the Acts emerged from the Catholic Bishops in the House of Lords (not from Puritans in the Commons, as Neale had argued — the key finding of Norman Jones's Faith by Statute, 1982), Elizabeth recalled Parliament after Easter, imprisoned some Catholic Bishops, and pushed the Act of Uniformity through the Lords by three votes; all Catholic Bishops voted against

### Elizabeth's Escalating Persecution of Catholics, 1569–1603
Shows Elizabeth using coercive state power against Catholics in a consistently reactive pattern — acting harshly only when Catholics threatened her hold on power, not from principled Protestant conviction alone.
- In the first decade (1558-68), recusancy fines were rarely enforced; Elizabeth's famous (though unrecorded) phrase "I will not make a window into men's souls" captures her policy of tolerating private Catholic belief in exchange for outward conformity
- The pattern shifted decisively after 1569: the Northern Rebellion, the Papal Bull of Excommunication (1570), the Ridolfi Plot (1571), and the arrival of seminary priests from 1574 all made Catholicism a direct political threat
- Three progressively more severe acts followed: the 1571 Act (making publication of papal bulls treasonable); the 1581 Act (making it treason to withdraw allegiance from Elizabeth or the Church, raising recusancy fines to £20 per month); the 1585 Act against Jesuits and Seminary Priests (making any priest ordained under papal authority guilty of treason by the mere fact of being in England — 123 priests were executed under this act between 1586 and 1603)
- Edmund Campion (Jesuit, arrived England 1580) was arrested and executed for treason in December 1581
- Babington Plot 1586: Anthony Babington of Derbyshire planned to murder Elizabeth, free Mary Stuart, and install her as queen with continental Catholic support; uncovered by Sir Francis Walsingham's secret service; led directly to Mary Stuart's execution in 1587
- Duke of Norfolk was executed for treason in March 1572 as a result of the Ridolfi Plot; the Spanish ambassador was expelled
- Elizabeth ordered the execution of 700 Northern rebels; only 450 were actually executed; by 1603, 180 Catholics had been executed in total, 120 of them priests
- The lesson note from the classroom materials makes the argument explicit: "Elizabeth treated Catholics more harshly when her power was threatened, so her priority was power rather than religious opposition to Catholicism"

### The Role of Longevity: Elizabeth's 45-Year Reign
The single most compelling Power argument for Elizabeth's reign — the Reformation's successful consolidation under Elizabeth owed more to her sheer longevity and political skill than to the inherent strength of Protestant ideas.
- Elizabeth reigned for 45 years (1558-1603) — compared to Edward's 6 and Mary's 5; the Elizabethan settlement had time to become the normal state of affairs for people who had never known anything else
- Had she died of smallpox in 1562 (she survived but was left temporarily bald), a Catholic succession was likely; the entire Protestant settlement would potentially have been reversed as rapidly as it had been reversed twice before
- By the end of the reign Patrick Collinson (The Religion of Protestants, 1982) notes that by about 1580 the established Church "had won control of the commanding heights of society" — but this was the result of sustained enforcement over more than two decades, not of any rapid popular conversion
- The Spanish Armada's defeat in 1588 removed the most serious external threat to the Protestant settlement; English national identity and Protestant identity became increasingly fused through the experience of successful resistance to Catholic Spain, creating a "common religion and common enemy" as the foundation of Protestant popular identity
- By the close of Elizabeth's reign, Catholicism had been reduced to "the religion of a small number of gentry — reduced to a sect" with widespread support only in a few counties such as Lancashire; Penry Williams's conclusion: "by about 1580 the established Church had won control of the commanding heights of society" — but this was achieved through enforcement, social pressure, and political management as much as through genuine theological conversion
- Murphy et al.'s verdict: "Her reign had been 30 years of illusion, followed by 15 years of disillusion" (quoting Haigh) — by 1603 few people gave the Queen much credit for what had been achieved; but the Protestant settlement itself had outlasted the controversies of her later years and was irreversibly consolidated

### The Baddesley Clinton Priest Hole Raid, 1591
A specific, vivid local case study illustrating both the persistence of Catholic conviction despite state coercion and the practical lengths to which that coercion drove committed Catholics — useful as concrete counter-evidence to any claim that Protestant ideas had won universal acceptance.
- In 1586 Henry Ferrers rented Baddesley Clinton in Warwickshire to the strongly Catholic Vaux sisters; they used the house to hide Catholic priests
- Nicholas Owen, a talented builder and lay Jesuit brother, constructed three priest holes in the house: one in the ceiling, one with a door camouflaged in wood panelling, and one accessible only down the toilet between two upstairs bedrooms, with the hiding space in the sewer below
- On a morning in 1591, priest-hunters (poursuivants) arrived at around 5am as Mass was being prepared; Father John Gerard and Father Henry Garnet were among the seven or eight priests and helpers present; loyal servants delayed the searchers at the gate, risking their lives; priests hid their boots, swords (Father Gerard was a skilled swordsman), and even turned their mattresses over so they would not be warm to the touch
- The search lasted four hours; the priest hunters left empty-handed (though with money given by the Vaux sisters); all priests escaped safely; there were likely other raids that went undocumented for obvious reasons
- Father Gerard was later arrested and sent to the Tower of London but escaped and fled to Europe, where he died of old age; Father Henry Garnet was arrested and executed in 1606 following the Gunpowder Plot; Nicholas Owen died under torture in 1606
- Thomas Percy (one of the Gunpowder Plotters) separately rented a house belonging to Ferrers in London and used it to store gunpowder
- This case study demonstrates directly that state power, however escalating its coercive measures, had not extinguished Catholic conviction among committed English Catholics by the 1590s; the extraordinary lengths to which Catholics went to preserve worship — elaborate concealed spaces, personal risk of execution — is evidence of genuine religious conviction, not merely inherited habit, surviving forty years of Protestant rule
